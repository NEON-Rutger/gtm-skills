# The RevOps to finance bridge

The most useful thing a commercial operator can hold in their head. It lets them argue in the CFO's units.

## The mapping

| Commercial lever or metric | Where it lands financially |
|---|---|
| New and expansion ARR | Revenue, recognized over the delivery period, not at signature |
| Churn and contraction | Revenue decline; churn also writes off the unamortized capitalized commission on the lost contract, contraction alone usually does not |
| Discounting | Gross margin and realized price. Shows up as price variance |
| Sales cycle length | Timing of revenue recognition, and working capital |
| Forecast accuracy | Working capital planning and covenant headroom predictability |
| Quota attainment | Variable compensation cost in the S&M line |
| Headcount plan | S&M and R&D opex, and compensation cost of sales |
| Services attach rate | Blended gross margin, usually downward |
| Billing terms (annual versus monthly) | Deferred revenue and operating cash flow |
| Payment terms conceded in negotiation | DSO, working capital, net debt |
| Collections and invoice disputes | DSO and cash conversion |
| Multi-year contracts | RPO, and commission amortization period |
| Commission plan design | Capitalized contract costs, and therefore EBITDA |
| Pipeline coverage | Nothing directly, which is why finance discounts it. Convert to expected revenue with historical conversion rates before presenting it |
| Win rate | Sales efficiency and CAC, via cost per won deal |
| Time to first value | Renewal probability, and therefore revenue retention |
| CRM data quality | Everything above, because it is the measurement layer for all of it |

## Reframing commercial asks

The pattern: name the financial line, quantify the movement, then state the ask. Every figure in the strong column illustrates the shape of the sentence; none is a benchmark. Substitute the company's own numbers before using any of them.

| Weak framing | Strong framing |
|---|---|
| "We need better forecast discipline" | "Forecast error of 20% forces a working capital buffer and makes covenant headroom unpredictable" |
| "Our CRM data is a mess" | "We cannot evidence the amortization period for capitalized commissions, which is an audit exposure" |
| "Sales keeps discounting" | "Realized price is down 6% year on year, which takes 6% of subscription revenue straight out of gross profit, entirely price, not volume" |
| "We need a commission tool" | "Manual calculation cannot produce a per-contract amortization schedule, so the contract-cost sub-ledger required by ASC 340-40 and IFRS 15 is a spreadsheet nobody has reconciled" |
| "We should tighten stage gates" | "Deals slipping rather than closing moves revenue across a covenant test date" |
| "We need more headcount" | "At current attainment and quota-to-OTE ratio, coverage requires four more reps to reach the plan, which adds two points to cost of sales as a share of revenue" |
| "Onboarding takes too long" | "Time to first value correlates with renewal, and a churned cohort also writes off its unamortized capitalized commissions" |

## What finance actually reports on, and why some commercial metrics do not travel

A CFO reports externally and to a board on revenue, margin, EBITDA, cash and net debt. Commercial metrics reach that conversation only when they map to one of those five.

**Metrics that travel well:** ARR and the ARR bridge, net and gross revenue retention, realized price, quota attainment, DSO, billing mix.

**Metrics that travel badly without translation:** pipeline coverage, MQLs, activity volumes, win rate in isolation, stage conversion rates, dashboard adoption.

The fix is not to abandon the second group. It is to convert them before presenting. Pipeline coverage becomes expected revenue when multiplied by historical stage conversion. Win rate becomes cost per won deal when combined with S&M spend. Activity becomes nothing at all, and should stay out of finance conversations.

## Working with a new or incoming CFO

A new CFO usually wants three things in their first hundred days: reliable numbers, a defensible forecast, and visibility into where revenue actually comes from. All three are revenue operations deliverables.

The highest-leverage offers, in order:

1. **An ARR bridge that reconciles to recognized revenue.** Most companies cannot produce this cleanly. Producing it makes the commercial function credible immediately.
2. **A forecast with a stated accuracy history.** Not a better forecast. A measured one. Error bars beat optimism.
3. **Consistent definitions across entities.** In a merged group, agreeing what ARR, churn and expansion mean is worth more than any dashboard, and nobody else will do it.

**What not to lead with:** a dashboard. Finance has seen many dashboards. They want a reconciliation.

## Two-way translation

The bridge runs both ways. When finance says something, translate it into what it means commercially.

| Finance says | It usually means |
|---|---|
| "We need to manage working capital" | Billing terms, payment terms or collections are hurting cash |
| "There is limited headroom" | A covenant is close and discretionary spend is about to be frozen |
| "The quality of earnings is a question" | Too much of profit rests on add-backs or capitalization choices |
| "We need to be careful about cut-off" | Deals recognized in the wrong period, usually at quarter end |
| "That is a P&L item, not a balance sheet item" | It hits profit now rather than being spread over years |
| "What is the recurring element?" | They are separating durable revenue from one-off revenue for valuation |
